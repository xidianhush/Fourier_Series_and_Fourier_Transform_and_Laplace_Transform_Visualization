Signals & Systems · Fourier / Laplace Visualization Collection

One storyline: a signal is a linear combination of complex exponentials. From the Fourier series of a periodic signal (a discrete sum over harmonics), to spectral lines growing denser and shorter as the period T→∞ — the Fourier transform (a continuous integral) — and finally to moving the integration onto the line s = σ+jω, which gives the inverse Laplace transform. Every page shows this "synthesis" as a chain of rotating, scaling vectors placed tip-to-tail in the complex plane, while the lower panel plots the projection of the chain tip onto the real axis as a function of t.

① Fourier Series Linear Superposition A periodic signal is a sum of harmonically related complex exponentials. Weighted rotating basis vectors join tip-to-tail in the complex plane; the real part of the chain tip's trajectory is the waveform. ② From Fourier Series to Fourier Transform As T grows, the spectral lines grow denser and F_n shorter: watch the discrete sum turn into (1/2\pi)\int F(j\omega)e^{j\omega t}\,d\omega. ③ Signal Synthesis by the Inverse Fourier Transform An aperiodic signal is a weighted integral of e^{j\omega t} spread over the whole \omega axis. The Riemann-sum vector chain moves in sync with the spectral lines, and the spectrum can switch to a 3D fence (standing on \sigma=0 — the \sigma axis is reserved for the inverse Laplace transform). ④ Signal Synthesis by the Inverse Laplace Transform The synthesis basis becomes e^{(\sigma+j\omega)t} and the weights X(\sigma+j\omega): a vector chain that scales as it rotates. In the 3D s-plane view at top right, the whole fence stands on the integration path \mathrm{Re}\,s=\sigma; bar height = weight, color = phase.
