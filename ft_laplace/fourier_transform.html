5. Understanding the Geometric Meaning of Fourier Transform & Spectral Density

Winding a signal around a circle at frequency f tells frequencies apart.
A frequency present in the signal bunches the curve on one side.
That pulls the center of mass away from the origin.
A frequency that is absent spreads the curve evenly.
The center of mass then stays near the origin.

That center of mass is what the Fourier transform measures.
Writing it down shows why the transform has that shape.

Sampling the signal

Sample the signal every \Delta t over the window -T/2 \to T/2.
This gives N sample points t_k = -T/2 + k\,\Delta t, each with a value f(t_k).
The samples and the step size are tied to the window by:

N \cdot \Delta t = T \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad \frac{\Delta t}{T} = \frac{1}{N}

The center of mass as a sum

Give the curve a total mass of 1.
The N samples sit in the window -T/2 \to T/2, each carrying the mass 1/N.
At step k the curve has wound to the point e^{-j 2 \pi f t_k}.
So the sample adds the mass 1/N at the position f(t_k)\,e^{-j 2 \pi f t_k}.
The center of mass is therefore:

\sum_{k=1}^{N} \frac{1}{N} f\left(t_{k}\right) e^{-j 2 \pi f t_{k}}

Taking the limit — a Riemann sum

Since 1/N = \Delta t / T, the sum is an average over [-T/2, T/2]:

\sum_{k=1}^{N} \frac{1}{N} f\left(t_{k}\right) e^{-j 2 \pi f t_{k}} = \sum_{k=1}^{N} \frac{\Delta t}{T} f\left(t_{k}\right) e^{-j 2 \pi f t_{k}}
= \frac{1}{T} \sum_{k=1}^{N} f\left(t_{k}\right) e^{-j 2 \pi f t_{k}} \Delta t

This is a Riemann sum.
The window [-T/2,T/2] is cut into N sub-intervals of length \Delta t.
On each one, take the point \xi_k = t_k.
This forms the product f(\xi_k)\,e^{-j 2 \pi f \xi_k}\,\Delta t.
If f(t) is bounded on [-T/2,T/2], the limit as \Delta t \to 0 exists:

\lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{1}{T} \sum_{k=1}^{N} f\left(t_{k}\right) e^{-j 2 \pi f t_{k}} \Delta t \longrightarrow \frac{1}{T} \int_{-T/2}^{T/2} f(t) e^{-j 2 \pi f t} \mathrm{d}t

An average, not a density

Watch the factor 1/T.
What we computed is the average position of the wound curve.
The mass was spread over the window, not over the curve itself.

For a periodic signal, every period draws the same figure.
The center of mass keeps a finite amplitude.
The average therefore settles on a definite value.
For a non-periodic signal, a longer T spreads the curve over more time.
The average is diluted and the center of mass creeps inward.
Its amplitude becomes infinitesimal as T \to \infty.

The information has not disappeared.
It was only divided by an ever larger T.
So the average is the wrong thing to keep.
What we want is the density, the center of mass per unit time.

The Fourier transform — scaling the center of mass up

Multiplying the average by T undoes the normalization.
The coordinate of the center of mass then grows with the interval.
This step also turns an amplitude in volts into a density.
That unit is V/Hz, an amplitude per unit frequency.
It is why the result is called a spectral density.

From a finite window to the whole time axis

T was only the length of our observation window.
It says nothing about the signal itself.
A transform should not remember it.
So let the window grow at both ends.
For a signal that dies out, the new samples carry no amplitude.
This exponential is down to about 0.02 when the window closes.
Past either end, the sample weight is 0 \cdot e^{-j 2 \pi f t_k} = 0.
That cannot move the center of mass.
Both ends therefore stretch out together, and T drops out of the result.
Then \int_{-T/2}^{T/2} becomes \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}, the two-sided transform.
It privileges no starting time.
A signal that never dies out, a periodic one say, differs.
Extending its window keeps adding fresh periods instead.
Letting t cover the whole line also lets f be negative.
That is the winding travelling the other way around the circle.
A real signal satisfies F(-f) = \overline{F(f)}.
Its spectrum is therefore conjugate symmetric about f = 0.
The two halves of the frequency axis are mirror images.
The default range here sweeps both of them, and Section 4 shows the symmetric hump.

With the window opened out, T has dropped out.
The definition of the Fourier transform reads:

F(j\omega) \stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(t) e^{-j \omega t} \mathrm{d}t, \qquad \omega = 2\pi f

Here \omega is the angular frequency for the winding frequency f.

What the transform tells you

The center of mass of the wound curve lies on the complex plane.
So F(j\omega) is in general a complex number.
Its magnitude |F(j\omega)| is how far the curve bunches to one side.
That carries the amplitude of the frequency component, in V/Hz.
Its phase angle \angle F(j\omega) is the direction it bunches in.
That carries the phase of the frequency component.
