7. Understanding the Laplace Transform

The Fourier page wound a signal around a circle at frequency $\omega$.
Its kernel was $e^{-j\omega t}$, a point of modulus one.
Turning a signal cannot emphasise any part of it: the weight is the same everywhere.
The Laplace transform adds a real exponent to that kernel, $s = \sigma + j\omega$.

$$ e^{-st} = e^{-\sigma t} \cdot e^{-j\omega t} $$

The second factor is the old rotation.
The first one is real and depends on $t$: it is an envelope, and it is new.
So the winding machine is unchanged; only the signal that feeds it is different.
It now feeds $f(t)e^{-\sigma t}$ instead of $f(t)$.

The definition

$$ F(s) \stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=} \int f(t)\, e^{-st} \,\mathrm{d}t, \qquad s = \sigma + j\omega $$

The integral is unilateral, $\int_0^\infty$, or bilateral, $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}$.
The switch in the input panel chooses which, and both are covered below.

Centroid, then integral

The derivation of Section 5 of the Fourier page carries over word for word.
Sample the window at $t_k$ with step $\Delta t$, put a mass $\frac{1}{N}$ on each sample, and the centroid of the wound curve is

$$ \sum_{k=1}^{N} \frac{1}{N}\, f(t_k)\, e^{-s t_k} $$

Since $\frac{1}{N} = \frac{\Delta t}{T}$, this is a Riemann sum for an average:

$$ \frac{1}{T}\sum_{k=1}^{N} f(t_k) e^{-s t_k} \Delta t \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{1}{T}\int_{\mathrm{window}} f(t) e^{-st}\,\mathrm{d}t $$

Multiplying by $T$ turns that average back into the integral.
It is the same step as on the Fourier page, and it produces the same unit of density.
The only thing that changed is the kernel.

What σ does

The envelope $e^{-\sigma t}$ weights the late part of the window against the early part.
For $\sigma > 0$ it shrinks the tail, for $\sigma < 0$ it inflates it, and for $\sigma = 0$ it does nothing.
So $\sigma$ is a knob for how much the far end of the observation window is allowed to count.

Setting $\sigma = 0$ leaves $F(j\omega)$, the Fourier transform of the Fourier page.
Everything on this page at $\sigma = 0$ is that page, seen from a slightly different angle.

Poles

Take the default signal, $f(t) = e^{-2t}$ on the unilateral window.
The integrand is $e^{-2t}e^{-\sigma t} = e^{-(\sigma+2)t}$.
At $\sigma = 0$ it decays, and the centroid settles on a finite number, about $0.49$ for this window.
At $\sigma = -1$ it still decays, only half as fast.
At $\sigma = -2$ the two exponentials cancel exactly and the integrand is constant.
The centroid then grows in proportion to the window: $T$ for a window of length $T$.
Push $\sigma$ below $-2$ and the integrand grows, so the centroid runs away exponentially in $T$.

That value, $s = -2$, is a pole.
It is the point where the kernel's own envelope can cancel the signal's decay.
For $s$ purely real and north of $-2$ the sum converges; at $s=-2$ it does not.
Nothing special is happening to the signal, only to the pair (signal, kernel).

Poles need not sit on the real axis.
Load the second preset, $f(t) = e^{-0.5t}\cos(2\pi t)$.
Its two decaying oscillations are matched by the kernel when $\sigma = -0.5$ and $\omega = \pm 2\pi$.
Inside the window the integrand then stops decaying and just oscillates, and the centroid grows like the window length.
That is the pole pair $s = -0.5 \pm j2\pi$, and those are the two bright ridges on the s-plane map.

Magnitude and phase

$|F(s)|$ is how far the wound curve bunches to one side, so it carries amplitude.
$\angle F(s)$ is the direction in which it bunches, so it carries phase.
A pole makes $|F(s)|$ unbounded, so the map shows a pole as a place where the picture saturates.
A zero would do the opposite, and shows up as a place where the map goes dark and the curve winds up neatly on the origin.

The region of convergence

Which $s$ make the integral finite?
For the unilateral integral it is a matter of the $t \to +\infty$ end, so it is a condition on $\sigma$ alone: a vertical half-plane, everything to the right of the rightmost pole.
For $f(t)=e^{-2t}$ that half-plane is $\sigma > -2$.

For the bilateral integral both ends have to decay, which gives a vertical strip.
With $f(t) = e^{-2|t|}$ the integral is

$$ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-2|t|} e^{-st}\,\mathrm{d}t = \frac{1}{s+2} + \frac{1}{2-s} = \frac{4}{4-s^2}, \qquad -2 < \sigma < 2 $$

Two poles at $s = \pm 2$ and a strip between them.
Outside the strip one of the two tails grows and the integral diverges, on whichever side the offending pole lies.
Widen σ max past $2$ in the panel, reload the preset $e^{-2|t|}$, and both edges of the strip appear.

A step function makes an even quicker example.
$f(t) = 1$ for $t \ge 0$ integrates to $1/s$, a single pole at the origin and the half-plane $\sigma > 0$.

What a finite window can and cannot show

This page never lets $T \to \infty$, and it should be honest about what that costs.

The first thing to know is that $|F|$ as a function of $\sigma$ is a ramp, not a ridge.
With a finite window the integrand is $f(t)e^{-\sigma t}$, and going further left in $\sigma$ keeps enlarging the far end of the window.
$|F|$ therefore rises monotonically as $\sigma$ decreases, and its maximum sits on the edge of the σ grid, not at the pole.
A map of $|F|$ in the σ direction answers "where am I most strongly amplifying the tail", which is only loosely "where is the pole".

The second thing is why the front looks sharp anyway.
Outside the region of convergence the growth per unit step in $\sigma$ is $e^{\,T_{\mathrm{map}}\Delta\sigma}$, and it does not depend on $\omega$ at all.
A ratio of about $54.6 = e^{4}$ for $\Delta\sigma = 0.2$ with $T_{\mathrm{map}} = 20$, at any $\omega$, is what makes the front a straight vertical line rather than a fan.
But that steepness is a property of the window length, not of the transform.
The map in Section 4 is a picture of $T_{\mathrm{map}}$, clipped to four decades; the region of convergence is the limit as $T \to \infty$.

Section 5 avoids the colour scale altogether.
It draws the same $|F|$ against $\sigma$ three times, for the windows $T$, $2T$ and $4T$.
Well inside the region of convergence the integrand has already died out by the end of the window, so all three windows give the same number and the curves lie on top of each other.
Outside it, a longer window collects more of a growing integrand, so the curves separate, and they separate faster the further out you go.
The point where they split is the pole's real part, read off without any choice of colours.

Reading the spiral

Section 6 unwinds the same sum in three dimensions.
The depth axis is time.
The thin spiral is the kernel $e^{-st}$: its radius is $e^{-\sigma t}$ and it turns at $-\omega$ radians per unit of $t$.
At $\sigma = 0$ the radius never changes and the kernel is a cylinder — that is the Fourier machine.
At $\sigma > 0$ it funnels inward toward the time axis, and at $\sigma < 0$ it flares outward.

The thick curve is the signal multiplied by that kernel, so it is the actual curve whose centroid is $F(s)$.
When the signal's own decay cancels the kernel's growth, the thick curve stops being pulled toward the axis and keeps its distance from it.
The centroid then climbs without bound, and that is a pole, seen directly.
At $s = -2 + 0j$ with the default signal, the whole window reduces to a straight ray leaving the origin, and the camera cannot help showing it.

No symbolic maths

Every number on this page comes from evaluating your function on a grid of samples over $t$ and summing.
Nothing is factored, solved or integrated symbolically.
Poles and regions of convergence here are never computed from a formula; they are what the pictures force you to conclude.
That is also why the numeric front on the map is not the same as the dashed line a textbook would draw.
